How to build a rocket

Getting to orbit means reaching about sideways, 200 km up, without the vehicle shaking apart, melting or running out of propellant. This guide covers what it takes: the roadmap, the physics, the engineering, the materials and the money. Falcon 9 is the reference throughout.

    02 · The hard science

    Why orbit is so hard

    Orbit isn’t about going up. It’s about going sideways fast enough that the ground curves away beneath you as fast as you fall towards it. All the difficulty comes from one equation.

    The rocket equation

    Δv = Isp · g0 · ln(m0 / mf)

    • Δv: the change in velocity the rocket can produce
    • Isp: specific impulse, a measure of engine efficiency in seconds (Merlin: 282 s at sea level, 348 s in vacuum)
    • g0: 9.81 m/s²
    • m0 / mf: full mass ÷ empty mass (the mass ratio)

    Because of the logarithm, each extra km/s costs exponentially more propellant. To reach about 9 km/s with kerosene engines, a single stage would need to be over 94% propellant by mass. That’s close to the limit of how thin a tank wall can be. This is why rockets have stages: you throw away empty tank mass partway up so you aren’t still accelerating it.

    The velocity budget

    To reach a 200 km orbit you need orbital speed plus everything lost on the way up. Launching east gives you Earth’s spin for free (−370 m/s from Florida).

    Total needed after the rotation credit: about 8,970 m/s. Loss figures are typical textbook values for a large liquid-fuelled rocket.

    Thrust and weight

    At liftoff, nine Merlins produce 7.6 MN against a weight of 5.4 MN, a thrust-to-weight ratio of about 1.4. As propellant burns off the rocket gets lighter, so acceleration rises. The engines throttle down near max-Q and again before cutoff to keep the load under about 4–5 g.

    Chemistry

    Kerosene (RP-1) burns with liquid oxygen at about 2.36 kg of oxygen per kg of fuel, reaching roughly 3,400 °C. The exhaust is mostly CO₂, CO and H₂O. Hydrogen would give a higher Isp, but it is 11 times less dense, so the tanks have to be much bigger. Kerosene keeps the rocket compact and cheap.

    Nozzles and air pressure

    A nozzle turns hot, high-pressure gas into speed. It works best when the exhaust leaves at the same pressure as the surrounding air. At sea level that means a short nozzle (expansion ratio about 16:1). In vacuum, a much bigger bell (about 165:1) gets more out of the same gas. That’s why the second-stage engine looks so different.

    Max-Q

    Dynamic pressure, ½ρv², peaks about 72 seconds in, when the rocket is fast but the air is still fairly thick. The structure is sized for this moment. Engines throttle down to get through it.

    Gravity turn

    The rocket rises vertically, then pitches over slightly and lets gravity bend its path towards horizontal. That keeps the angle of attack, and so the sideways aerodynamic load, near zero. Climbing too long wastes Δv on gravity losses. Turning too early wastes it on drag and heating.

    Coming back down

    To land, the booster flips over, may burn back towards the launch site, relights three engines to slow down before thick air (the entry burn), then uses one engine to touch down. A Merlin can’t throttle low enough to hover, so the landing burn is timed to reach zero speed exactly at zero height.

    03 · Engineering

    The engine is the rocket

    Merlin 1D is a gas-generator engine. A small amount of propellant is burned to drive a turbine, which spins the pumps that push the rest into the main chamber at high pressure. That turbine exhaust is then dumped overboard: a little less efficient, but much simpler to build than closed-cycle designs.

    Propulsion

    Nine engines on the first stage give engine-out capability and lots of flight data from a single design. Engines gimbal (tilt) to steer. TEA-TEB, a pyrophoric fluid that ignites on contact with air, lights them, and they can be restarted in flight.

    Structures

    The tanks are pressurised with helium, which also makes them stiffer. A common dome separates LOX from RP-1. Propellants are densified (LOX at −207 °C, RP-1 at −7 °C) so the same tanks hold about 10% more.

    Avionics and software

    Triple-redundant flight computers vote on every decision. Guidance re-plans the trajectory in real time. If anything fails, an autonomous flight termination system can end the flight safely without anyone on the ground.

    Guidance for landing

    Landing is solved on board as a convex optimisation problem: find the engine commands that land on the target using the least fuel. Grid fins steer in thick air, and cold-gas nitrogen thrusters control attitude in thin air.

    Ground systems

    Stages are integrated horizontally in a hangar. A transporter-erector lifts the rocket and supplies propellant, power and data until liftoff. Propellant loading starts only 35 minutes before launch, so the densified propellant stays cold.

    Testing

    Test the way you fly. Every engine is hot-fired before delivery, every stage is static-fired, and the vehicle does a short static fire on the pad before its first flight. Most problems are caught in testing, not in flight.
